# Murder in Mussoorie — Game Bible

**A Cooperative Murder Mystery Party Game**
**For 6–8 Players | Ages 10+**
**Designed by Shashank Mendiratta · May 2026 · Version 1.0**

> **Spoiler warning**: This document contains the full solution. For player-facing materials, see `printables/`. The canonical source is also kept as `Game_Bible.docx` alongside this file.

---

## 1. Game overview

### Setting

Mussoorie, Uttarakhand, India. Present day. A misty hill station perched on the Himalayan foothills — Mall Road glowing under streetlamps at night, the winding emptiness of Camel's Back Road, the old colonial cottages of Landour half-hidden in cedar forests, the silence of Gun Hill after dark. A town where everyone knows everyone, and secrets don't stay buried forever.

### Tone

Family-friendly (ages 10+). No gore, no romance, no substance abuse. Think Agatha Christie energy — dark themes handled lightly and cleverly. The mystery is intellectual, not visceral. Death is referenced but never described graphically. The emotional core is about loyalty, betrayal, fear, and the lengths people go to protect what they love.

### The Hook

The players are a **CBI (Central Bureau of Investigation) special team** dispatched from Dehradun. They've been sent to Mussoorie because an anonymous letter alleges that the recent death of a popular YouTuber — ruled accidental by local police — was actually murder, and that the investigating officer himself is a suspect. The local cops don't want the CBI here. Nobody in town is happy to see them asking questions.

As the investigation unfolds, the players discover they're not just solving one murder — they're uncovering a **fifteen-year-old cold case** that connects to everything.

## 2. The cold case — the Thakur family (15 years ago)

### The Thakur family

**Brigadier Ranjit Thakur** (retired) and his wife **Meera** were old Mussoorie money. They owned **Thakur Cottage**, a sprawling colonial-era bungalow in Landour — one of the finest properties in the hill station. The Brigadier was respected but feared. He was the kind of man who used his army connections and social status like a weapon — powerful friends in the district administration, a voice that carried weight in every local dispute.

The Brigadier also had an elderly mother who lived with them. Three generations under one roof, in a house that had been in the family for decades.

### The feud: Thakur vs. Bisht

Fifteen years ago, a small-time hotelier named **Harish Bisht** owned a modest lodge near Landour Bazaar. A land boundary dispute arose between the Thakur estate and Bisht's lodge. It should have been a minor civil matter. But the Brigadier treated it as an affront. He used his army connections, his friendships with district officials, and his money to get Bisht's hotel licence revoked. It was ruthless and disproportionate — like using a cannon to swat a fly.

Harish Bisht lost everything. His hotel, his income, his dignity. He died six months later — heart attack, though everyone in town knew it was a broken man giving up.

He left behind a 22-year-old son: **Rajveer Bisht** — the man the town now knows as **Mr. Bisht**, Mussoorie's most successful hotelier. Young Rajveer watched his father crumble and die. He didn't make a scene. He didn't swear revenge in public. He just waited. And planned.

### The crime: a staged robbery

One cold December night, Thakur Cottage was broken into. The official story: thieves targeted the Brigadier's known collection — his army service medals, his wife's gold jewelry, and a prized British-era Enfield rifle that had been in the family since the Raj. The Thakurs surprised the intruders. Things turned violent. The Brigadier was struck on the head. His wife and mother, who tried to intervene, were also attacked. All three died. The cottage was ransacked. The thieves were never caught. Case went cold.

### The truth

There were no thieves. Rajveer Bisht hired two men from outside Mussoorie — migrant labourers passing through, no local connections, no paper trail. They weren't there to steal. They were there to kill. But the staging had to look like a robbery gone wrong, so they broke locks, scattered belongings, and took a few items — the jewelry, the medals, and the rifle — to make it look convincing. The items were supposed to be disposed of. The two men left town that same night and were never seen in Mussoorie again.

The investigating officer was a young, underpaid sub-inspector named **Devraj Khanna**. Bisht had approached him weeks before the crime. The deal: ensure the investigation goes nowhere. Chase phantom leads for a few weeks, then let the file gather dust. In return, Devraj would receive enough money to clear his debts and a promise of ongoing "friendship." Devraj didn't help plan the robbery. But when it happened, he knew exactly what it was — and he looked the other way.

### The profit

After the Thakurs died, the estate went into legal limbo. No direct heirs. Distant relatives filed claims, but the cases dragged on for years in Indian courts. Meanwhile, Bisht — patiently building a small property business — began acquiring land around Thakur Cottage through proxy buyers and shell companies. Not the cottage itself (too obvious), but adjacent plots, access roads, nearby properties.

Over fifteen years, Bisht transformed himself from the bankrupt hotelier's son into Mussoorie's biggest property developer. He built the **Royal Pines Hotel** — the town's most successful luxury hotel — on land that the Thakurs once owned. He never bought Thakur Cottage itself. It still sits there, locked and decaying. A monument to a crime no one knows about.

One item Bisht kept: the **British-era Enfield rifle**. It was supposed to be destroyed. But Bisht couldn't let it go. It was his trophy — the symbol of the family that destroyed his father, now hanging on his wall. He mounted it in his private office at the Royal Pines, displayed as a "personal collection piece." Fifteen years had passed. Nobody remembered the Thakur inventory. He thought he was untouchable.

### The silent witness: Anya Devi

**Anya Devi** was the Thakurs' housekeeper. She'd worked for the family for five years. On the night of the robbery, she wasn't in the cottage — she'd gone home to her village for her son's school function. But in the week before, she noticed things. Two unfamiliar men near the cottage when the family was out. A window latch that had been tampered with. Small things that didn't seem important until the family was dead.

After the killings, Anya went to the police. She spoke to young Sub-Inspector Devraj Khanna. He took her statement, told her he'd look into it, and then the statement disappeared from the file. A week later, Bisht visited Anya at her home. He was polite. He offered to pay for her son **Rohan**'s schooling — "the Thakurs would have wanted that." The message was clear without being spoken: stay quiet, and your son has a future. Make noise, and you have nothing.

Anya took the deal. For fifteen years, she kept silent. Her son Rohan went to good schools on Bisht's money. He's now studying at the **Dehradun Institute of Technology**, a 30-minute drive from home. Anya continued working as a housekeeper in Mussoorie — eventually working for **Vikram Singh**, the YouTuber who rented a cottage just down the road from the burned-out Thakur house.

## 3. The victim — Vikram Singh

### Who was Vikram?

**Vikram Singh, 32**, was Mussoorie's most famous son. He grew up here, left for Delhi after college, then returned three years ago to start a YouTube channel called **"Hidden Mussoorie."** What began as gorgeous drone shots of forgotten trails and crumbling British-era ruins grew into a 400K-subscriber sensation. Vikram became the town's unofficial ambassador — charming, well-connected, invited everywhere.

But Vikram had a flaw. He couldn't leave a mystery alone. Over time, his channel shifted from "hidden places" to "hidden truths." He started seeing people's secrets as content. He was never cruel — he genuinely believed in transparency, in sunlight as disinfectant — but he was careless. He didn't fully grasp that some secrets, if dragged into the light, would destroy lives. Or end his own.

### How Vikram found the Thakur secret

Six weeks before his death, Vikram was filming an episode called "Forgotten Homes of Landour" for his channel. One house captivated him — **Thakur Cottage**. The decaying colonial bungalow with its broken locks, overgrown garden, and ransacked rooms frozen in time. Great footage.

Vikram needed to get inside. He asked around town and learned that his own housekeeper, Anya Devi, had once worked for the Thakurs. He approached Anya's son, Rohan — a college student at DIT Dehradun who still had his mother's old keys to the cottage. Vikram offered Rohan a spot in his documentary. A college kid offered screen time on a 400K-subscriber channel? Of course Rohan said yes. He grabbed his mother's old keys, walked Vikram through the cottage, and told him whatever stories he'd heard growing up: "This is where the Brigadier kept his medals," "This is where the thieves broke in," "Ma still keeps the old keys, she never throws anything away."

Vikram filmed everything. And then he noticed something. On the wall of the Brigadier's study, there were two empty hooks — a wall mount where something long and heavy had clearly hung for years. The outline was visible in the unfaded wallpaper. Rohan told him: "That's where the Brigadier's English rifle used to be. The thieves took it. Very old, very valuable."

Vikram photographed the empty mount. That photograph would eventually kill him.

### The investigation deepens

Vikram did what Vikram always did — he pulled the thread. He went to the district magistrate's office. He pulled the old robbery-murder FIR. The file was suspiciously thin — three pages for a triple homicide. No forensic report. No fingerprint analysis. No follow-up on the stolen items. The investigating officer's name: **Sub-Inspector Devraj Khanna**.

He checked pawn shop records and black market channels — none of the stolen items had ever surfaced. Stolen army medals and a British-era rifle are collector's items. They'd leave a trail. But there was no trail. Because the items were never sold.

Weeks later, Vikram visited the Royal Pines Hotel to interview Mr. Bisht for a different segment of his channel. In Bisht's private office, behind the reception desk, he spotted a British-era Enfield rifle mounted on the wall. His breath caught. Same make. Same era. Same distinctive carved stock. He photographed it discreetly. Later, he compared the two photos — the empty hooks at Thakur Cottage and the rifle at Royal Pines — and pulled the original theft FIR. The rifle's description matched exactly.

He pulled land registry records. Found the shell companies that bought up the Thakur-adjacent land. One of them — **"Highland Properties"** — traced back through two layers of ownership to a trust controlled by Rajveer Bisht. He researched Bisht's family history and found the Brigadier's role in destroying Harish Bisht. Revenge and profit — the oldest motives in the book.

Vikram now had the outline of a fifteen-year-old murder conspiracy. He put it all into his upcoming exposé: **"The Truth About Mussoorie."**

### The exposé video

The video wasn't just about Bisht and the Thakurs. Vikram had been collecting secrets on half the town. "The Truth About Mussoorie" was a bomb that would go off in every direction:

- **The Thakur cold case**: evidence connecting Bisht to the staged robbery and the shell companies that profited from the Thakur land.
- **Inspector Devraj's corruption**: footage and documents showing Devraj taking bribes from illegal construction projects around Mussoorie.
- **Rhea's embezzlement**: still unverified at the time of his death. Three weeks earlier, Vikram had quietly asked Naina Kapoor — his ex-fiancée, a corporate-investigations journalist with whom he'd remained friends — to dig into Rhea and a Delhi acquirer called Metropolis Media. Naina delivered a sourced memo nine days before he died. The memo confirmed a clandestine channel sale in motion and named the M&A contact, but couldn't confirm the embezzlement without internal documents; Naina explicitly told him to pull the Stripe statements himself. He read the memo, scrawled 'pull stripe' in the margin, and never followed up — he was drowning in the Thakur thread. The exposé video would have mentioned Rhea *if* he could confirm the books. He died before he confirmed.
- **Kabir's plagiarism**: evidence that his old college friend Prof. Kabir Iyer had plagiarised his PhD thesis — a thesis Vikram had helped him write years ago.
- **The Bisht land grab**: Bisht had secretly purchased the land under Vikram's own rented cottage and was planning to evict him.

Everyone had a reason to want the video killed. But only one secret was worth killing for.

### What Vikram left behind

Vikram did not die mid-thought. He worked the way investigative journalists work — *out loud*, in fragments, in places he could come back to. What the CBI can reconstruct is layered: round 1 surfaces what's *visible at the cottage* (his social-media trail, his working wall, his 11 PM voice memo); deeper artifacts surface in round 2 when specific suspects open up.

1. **His working wall (round 1)** — a corkboard in his study with three names on the spine (Bisht, Devraj, Anya) connected by red string to a 2011 Doon Echo cutting on the Thakur murders, plus a small "loose threads" corner with Kabir's name on a sticky note. The wall is the *visual* artifact: it shows the players exactly which threads Vikram had pulled and which he had not. The Bisht-to-Devraj connection — the one that mattered — is the line he never drew.
2. **A voice memo (round 1)** — recorded at 11:42 PM, two nights before his death, cutting off mid-sentence when his phone hit 1% battery. He is alone, thinking out loud, naming every suspect with a partial mix of certainty, suspicion, and unfinished thought. It is the *audio* artifact: the dead man speaking to the players in his own voice about the people he was watching.
3. **His phone (surfaces during Naina's round-2 interview)** — a thirty-day WhatsApp thread with Naina (twenty-three unanswered calls, the unread follow-ups) and her two-page corporate memo on Rhea / Metropolis Media with his marginal note 'pull stripe'. These artifacts come to light when the CBI questions Naina about her work and her relationship with Vikram; they are not visible at the cottage scene. Mechanically: cooperation-tier `unlockBehavior` on both artifacts, firing on the interviewer asking a reasonable professional question.

These artifacts are deliberately *partial*. Vikram was a fallible journalist, not an omniscient narrator. The wall has wrong strings and missing strings. The memo has lines like *"I haven't decided"* and *"I owe Naina a call."* The phone shows him ignoring crucial information delivered by a friend. The case design hangs on this fallibility: the players' job in rounds 2–4 is to finish a deduction Vikram was halfway through when he died.

## 4. The Grey Lady

Two weeks before his death, Anya overheard Vikram on a phone call while she was cleaning his cottage. He was talking to someone about "the Thakur fire" and "land records that don't add up." Her blood went cold. She'd spent fifteen years burying this. Now her own employer was about to dig it all up.

But Anya's fear wasn't simple. She wasn't afraid for herself — she was afraid for Vikram. She knew what Bisht was capable of. He'd had an entire family killed and walked away clean. If Vikram got close, Bisht would destroy him.

Every evening after her cleaning shift, instead of going home, Anya walked to Camel's Back Road. She stood in the mist, wrapped in her grey wool shawl, watching Vikram set up his camera, film his sunset shots, pack up. She wanted to warn him. She wanted to say: *Stop. You don't know what you're walking into. These men will kill you.* But she couldn't — because warning Vikram meant admitting she'd known about the Thakur murders for fifteen years and said nothing. It meant losing everything Bisht had given her. It meant her son learning his education was funded with blood money.

So she just watched. Night after night. A grey figure in the fog.

Vikram noticed. He didn't recognise her — why would he look closely at a distant figure on a misty road? He processed it through the lens of local ghost legends. Mussoorie has a famous folklore about a woman in grey seen walking Camel's Back Road at dusk. Vikram posted Instagram stories about it: *"Day 5. She's back. Mussoorie's Grey Lady doesn't want me on her road."* His followers loved it. He mentioned it to Rhea on a call. He told Kabir about it at the chai shop.

He never once considered that the "ghost" was his housekeeper, trying and failing to save his life.

## 6. The aftermath — the CBI arrives

### The first anonymous letter

Two weeks after Vikram's death, the CBI's Dehradun office receives an anonymous letter:

> "Vikram Singh's death was not accidental. The investigating officer, Inspector Devraj Khanna, is himself a suspect with motive to bury the case. Request independent investigation."

This letter was sent by **Anya Devi**. After the murder, she heard the "accidental death" ruling and understood exactly what had happened. She realised what she'd done — by going to Bisht, she'd effectively signed Vikram's death warrant. The guilt is eating her alive. But she still can't come forward directly — she's complicit. She was Bisht's source. She's been taking his money for fifteen years. So she sends the letter anonymously. It's the only act of courage she can manage.

The CBI dispatches a special investigation team. **The players are that team.**

### The second anonymous letter (midgame)

During the investigation (Round 3), the CBI team receives a second anonymous letter:

> "You're looking at the wrong suspects. Ask who benefits from the Thakur estate."

This letter was sent by **Prof. Kabir Iyer**. Kabir knows about the Thakur connection because Vikram told him at the chai shop the evening of the murder. He's been wrestling with his conscience. He can't come forward openly because the investigation would expose his plagiarism. But he can nudge the CBI toward the real motive.

The CBI now has two anonymous helpers — neither knows about the other. Both are cowards in their own way. Both are trying to do the right thing without paying the price for it.

## 7. The six suspects

### 1. Rhea Bhatia — the business partner

- **Public story**: "We were equal partners. I'm devastated. Vikram was my best friend and co-creator."
- **Secret**: Rhea had been embezzling revenue from the YouTube channel for over a year. She was also secretly negotiating to sell the channel to a Delhi media company — a deal that would make her rich but that Vikram would never have agreed to.
- **What she did that night**: Nothing — she was home all evening (phone records confirm it). But at 5:00 AM the next morning, before the body was found, she entered Vikram's cottage with her spare key and wiped the memory card and deleted research files to destroy evidence of her embezzlement.
- **Breaking point**: Building CCTV footage showing her entering Vikram's cottage at 5:00 AM. A draft email on her laptop showing the channel sale negotiation, dated two days before the murder.
- **Role in the story**: Evidence tamperer. Classic red herring — she inherits the channel, she destroyed evidence, she has financial motive. But she didn't kill anyone.

### 2. Inspector Devraj Khanna — the corrupt cop

- **Public story**: "I investigated thoroughly. It was a tragic accident. Wet path, poor visibility, old railing. These things happen in the hills."
- **Secret**: Devraj is the man who physically killed Vikram — struck him with his lathi and pushed him over the railing. He's also the man who covered up the Thakur robbery-murders fifteen years ago. He's been Bisht's enforcer for his entire career.
- **What he did that night**: Received Bisht's phone call at 8:00 PM. Drove to Camel's Back Road. Intercepted Vikram under the pretence of a safety escort. Killed him at the bend near Gun Hill. Drove back to the station and falsified his duty log.
- **Breaking point**: CCTV footage from a shop near Camel's Back Road showing his jeep at 8:10 PM — contradicting his claim of being at the station all evening. The lathi mark on Vikram's body (a detail his own cursory post-mortem conveniently omitted). Phone records showing an incoming call from Bisht's number at 8:00 PM.
- **Role in the story**: The killer (executor). He did the physical act. When cornered, his behaviour depends on the players' path (see Section 9: Endgame).

### 3. Naina Kapoor — the ex-fiancée and the corporate investigator

- **Day job**: Delhi-based corporate-investigations journalist who takes freelance design contracts on the side. The hybrid is a real thing in Delhi media circles. The design work pays the rent; the investigations work is what she's actually good at.
- **Relationship to Vikram**: They'd been engaged for two years; *he* broke it off four months ago. They had been trying to stay friends, and it had been working. About three weeks before his death, Vikram asked her — off the record, friend to friend — to dig quietly into his business partner Rhea and a Delhi acquirer called Metropolis Media. She delivered a sourced two-page memo nine days before he died. He read it. He did not reply. Then he did not reply to anything else either.
- **Public story**: "We broke up months ago — amicably, mostly. I was in town for a freelance design job. I was at my hotel that evening. I wasn't anywhere near Camel's Back Road."
- **Secret**: She was the friend who had just done him a serious favour and watched him go silent. The 23 calls in 48 hours are a collapse of two things into one — a professional follow-up that wasn't being answered and a friendship that wasn't either, until she could no longer separate them. She came up to Mussoorie that week to find out why and to try to make it not be the last thing between them. She walked Camel's Back Road that evening hoping to engineer an "accidental" encounter.
- **What she did that night**: She was on Camel's Back Road at approximately 8:20 PM. She heard something — a metallic sound, perhaps a cry. She did not investigate. She walked away.
- **Breaking point**: Phone records showing 23 calls to Vikram. A shopkeeper's statement placing her at the Kulri Bazaar end of Camel's Back Road at 8:15 PM. Both together force her to admit the road *and* the sound.
- **Role in the story**: An innocent bystander who might have saved Vikram if she'd investigated the sound. Her guilt is moral, not criminal. She is also the case's most important *narrative* engine in round 2 — when the players treat her as a witness rather than a suspect, she opens up and surfaces the corporate memo on Rhea (which is what gives the investigation its first concrete lead against Rhea) and the WhatsApp thread (which contextualises the 23 calls). She is the woman the dead man asked for help and then stopped answering, and she carries the weight of both being ignored and walking past the sound.

### 4. Mr. Rajveer Bisht — the hotelier

- **Public story**: "Yes, Vikram and I had business disagreements. But I'm a businessman, not a criminal. I was at my hotel all evening — check the CCTV."
- **Secret**: Bisht is the mastermind behind both murders. He had the Thakur family killed fifteen years ago for revenge (they destroyed his father) and profit (their land built his hotel empire). He ordered Vikram's murder when he learned the exposé was about to connect him to the Thakur cold case.
- **What he did that night**: Received Anya's warning at 7:30 PM. Called Devraj at 8:00 PM and gave the order. He never left the hotel himself. His CCTV shows him in the lobby — but there's a 15-minute gap (8:00–8:15 PM) when he stepped into his private office to make the call from a phone that couldn't be traced to the hotel switchboard.
- **Breaking point**: The British-era rifle in his office matching the Thakur theft inventory. Land registry documents showing shell companies tracing back to his trust. Phone records showing his call to Devraj at 8:00 PM. The CCTV gap. And the Bisht family history — his father's destruction at the Brigadier's hands, establishing the revenge motive.
- **Role in the story**: The mastermind. He never gets his hands dirty. That's what Devraj is for. His hotel, his reputation, his charm — they're all built on bones.

### 5. Anya Devi — the housekeeper

- **Public story**: "I just clean the houses. I don't know anything about Vikram's work or his video. I left the cottage at my usual time and went home."
- **Secret**: Anya is the tragic fulcrum of the entire story. She witnessed suspicious activity before the Thakur robbery fifteen years ago. She was silenced by Bisht with money for her son's education. She inadvertently triggered Vikram's murder by warning Bisht about the laptop. She is the **Grey Lady** who haunted Camel's Back Road. And she is the **anonymous tipster** who brought the CBI to Mussoorie.
- **What she did that night**: Saw Vikram's research on his laptop at 7:18 PM. Went to Bisht at the Royal Pines at 7:30 PM. Caught the 9:15 PM bus home. Did not know the murder was happening. Two weeks later, sent the anonymous letter to the CBI.
- **Breaking point**: Her timeline doesn't add up — she claims she left the cottage at 7:30 PM and went to the bus stand, but the bus stand is a 10-minute walk and her bus was at 9:15 PM. Where was she for over an hour? (She was at the Royal Pines, talking to Bisht.) When confronted with this and the evidence of Bisht's payments for Rohan's education, she breaks.
- **Role in the story**: The key witness. When she finally tells the truth, she ties everything together — the Thakur cold case, the cover-up, the warning to Bisht, and the Grey Lady. She is both victim and accidental accomplice.

### 6. Prof. Kabir Iyer — the college friend

- **Public story**: "I was visiting Mussoorie for nostalgia. Old college friends catching up. We had chai, talked about the old days. That's all."
- **Secret**: Kabir didn't come for nostalgia. He came because Vikram told him the exposé would mention his plagiarised PhD thesis. Kabir's entire academic career is built on work Vikram helped him write in college. He came to beg Vikram to remove it.
- **What he did that night**: Had a heated argument with Vikram at Lovely Omelette Centre at 6:30 PM. Vikram told him about the Thakur discovery. After the argument, Kabir went back to his hotel and didn't leave. Two weeks later, he sent the second anonymous letter to the CBI.
- **Breaking point**: A pawn-shop receipt showing Kabir sold his expensive watch the day before his trip — he was desperately raising money to "pay Vikram off." The chai-shop owner's testimony about the heated argument. Handwriting analysis matching the second anonymous letter.
- **Role in the story**: A coward but not a criminal. His worst act is putting self-preservation above truth. He's also the second anonymous tipster, trying to do the right thing without paying for it.

## 8. The Grey Lady — clue thread

The Grey Lady is one of the game's most distinctive elements — an atmospheric thread that feels supernatural but resolves into something deeply human. Here is how it appears across the game:

- **Clue 1 (Round 1)** — Vikram's Instagram stories — printable as phone screenshots. Three posts over two weeks referencing "the Grey Lady of Camel's Back Road." Players receive these as scene-setting material and think "spooky atmosphere."
- **Clue 2 (Round 1)** — Rhea's testimony: "He kept joking about a ghost. I told him to stop being dramatic. Now I wonder if someone was actually following him." Plants the seed: ghost or stalker?
- **Clue 3 (Round 2)** — Chai-shop owner's statement: "Haan sahib, I saw a woman walking that side many evenings. Grey shawl. I thought she was going to the temple." A local witness who saw Anya but didn't think anything of it.
- **Clue 4 (Round 3)** — Anya's grey shawl is mentioned in her belongings inventory as "freshly washed." Why wash a wool shawl in May? Unless you're erasing where you've been.
- **Resolution** — When Anya confesses, the Grey Lady is explained. She was never a ghost. She was a mother pacing a misty road for two weeks, watching a man walk toward his death, unable to find the words to save him.

## 9. The endgame — two paths to the truth

The game reaches its climax when the CBI team believes they've identified the killer(s). The interrogation plays out differently depending on who the players confront first. Both paths lead to the same truth, but the emotional journey differs.

### Path A: players identify Bisht first

Bisht is smooth. He doesn't panic. He puts on a masterful performance:

> "I'm the victim here. Inspector Devraj has been extorting me for years. He discovered my father's history with the Thakurs and threatened to frame me for their deaths unless I kept paying him. I've been trapped. When Vikram started digging into the Thakur case, Devraj panicked — not to protect me, but to protect himself. His cover-up of the original investigation would have been exposed. He killed Vikram on his own. I had nothing to do with it."

This is dangerously convincing because it's half true. Devraj did cover up the original case. Devraj is corrupt. And Bisht really does look like a man being squeezed by a dirty cop.

The players then go to Devraj with this story. Devraj realises Bisht is sacrificing him — fifteen years of loyalty, and Bisht is throwing him away like a used tool. That betrayal breaks him. He snaps:

> "He's lying. He planned all of it. The Thakurs. Vikram. Everything. He called me that night and told me where Vikram would be. I was always just his weapon."

### Path B: players identify Devraj first

Devraj is not Bisht. He's not smooth. He's a career follower who has never had to face consequences. When the CBI corners him with the evidence — the CCTV of his jeep, the lathi mark, the phone records, the timeline — he crumbles fast. Self-preservation kicks in immediately:

> "It wasn't my idea. None of it was ever my idea. Bisht made me do it. Fifteen years ago, the Thakurs — that was him. And Vikram — he called me that night, told me the boy knew everything, told me to handle it. I'm just a constable who got in too deep."

This path gives the players the satisfaction of watching a chain of cowardice collapse under its own weight. Devraj gives up Bisht immediately to save himself.

### The full truth (both paths)

Regardless of path, the full picture emerges: **Bisht is the mastermind** who ordered both the Thakur murders and Vikram's killing. **Devraj is the enforcer** who carried out both acts and covered them up using his police authority. They've been shackled together for fifteen years — neither able to betray the other without going down themselves. Until the CBI forced the issue.

Anya's confession completes the picture: the fifteen years of silence, the blood money for her son's education, the Grey Lady vigil, the warning to Bisht that triggered the murder, and the anonymous letter that brought the CBI.

## 9b. Round 2 scenario playbook (host reference)

Round 2 introduces the free-choice suspect picker on the TV. The host (or any player holding interviewer control) can interview any of the six suspects in any order and revisit at will. Each suspect's transcript and unlock state are preserved across re-entries by the engine.

This playbook lists which handoffs actually *fire engine cues* in round 2 (vs. which are deliberately stonewalled until round 3+). Use it to set host expectations.

### Cues that fire with the round-2 evidence drop

By the time the host advances past `r2-evidence-drop`, the locker contains: the round-1 set (Vikram's wall, voice memo, Instagram, YouTube channel, anonymous letter #1, police report, crime-scene summary) plus seven round-2 items: `chai-shop-receipt`, `naina-phone-records`, `shopkeeper-statement-naina`, `building-cctv-rhea`, `rhea-draft-email`, `bisht-hotel-cctv-log`, `pawn-shop-receipt`.

Five conditions can fire in round 2:

| Suspect | Condition | Tier | Required to fire |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naina | `secret:points-to-rhea` | cooperation | A reasonable professional question about her work, Vikram, Rhea, Metropolis, or Mussoorie this week. No evidence needed. |
| Naina | `secret:obsessive-calls` | cooperation | Asking about the calls; presenting `naina-phone-records` is the natural way. |
| Naina | `secret:heard-the-sound` + BP `shopkeeper-and-phone` | compound | Present `naina-phone-records` AND `shopkeeper-statement-naina`, then ask about Camel's Back Road / the timeline / what she heard. |
| Rhea | `secret:embezzlement` + BP `morning-cctv` | compound | Present `building-cctv-rhea` or `rhea-draft-email`, then ask about the channel sale, the 5 AM cottage visit, or Metropolis specifically. |
| Kabir | `secret:plagiarism` + BP `chai-and-pawn` | compound | Present `pawn-shop-receipt` (and ideally `chai-shop-receipt`), then ask politely about his PhD / college days / the chai-shop argument. No threats. |

### The canonical cooperation→evidence handoff

**Naina → Rhea (channel sale).** This is the chain you can build the round-2 experience around.

1. Players pick Naina. Ask any reasonable professional question — *"What do you do?"*, *"What brings you to Mussoorie?"*, *"Were you doing work for Vikram?"* — and `points-to-rhea` fires.
2. Naina names **Metropolis Media** and her **two-page memo** on Rhea. The corporate memo + WhatsApp thread land in the evidence locker.
3. Players switch to Rhea via the picker.
4. Players present `rhea-draft-email` (already in locker from r2-evidence-drop) and ask about the sale or Metropolis specifically.
5. Rhea's `embezzlement` fires: she admits selling the channel, taking a cut for a year.
6. Optional: present `building-cctv-rhea` and her `morning-cctv` BP fires — she admits the 5 AM cottage entry.

This is the design's flagship round-2 chain. Other handoffs in round 2 are softer (narrative context, not mechanical cue chains).

### Suspects who deliberately stonewall in round 2

Three suspects are *designed* to give nothing crackable in round 2. Players will hit deflections in their persona voice but no `unlockBehavior` will fire. This is correct — these reveals are paced for round 3+:

- **Devraj** — every secret and breakingPoint requires round-3 (`bisht-family-history`, `land-registry`) or round-4 (`devraj-jeep-cctv`, `lathi-postmortem`) evidence. Round 2 Devraj is a wall.
- **Bisht** — every condition requires round-3 (`wall-mount-photo`, `office-rifle-photo`, `land-registry`, `bisht-family-history`) or round-4 (`bisht-devraj-call`, `anya-payments`) evidence. Even with all of those, his `pressureThreshold: 4+` means he typically reframes rather than confesses; the endgame handles his unmasking.
- **Anya** — every condition requires round-3 (`anya-bus-ticket`) or round-4 (`anya-payments`, `grey-shawl-fresh`) evidence. Round 2 Anya looks at the floor.

Host tip: if players seem stuck on these three in round 2, that's the case telling them they don't yet have what they need. Direct them back to Naina/Rhea/Kabir or forward to the round-3 reveal.

### Soft handoffs (narrative context, no new cue fires)

Even when no new cue fires on re-entry, the engine preserves prior transcripts. These soft handoffs add context to subsequent interviews:

- **Kabir → Vikram-was-investigating-everyone framing.** Kabir's plagiarism reveal explicitly mentions Vikram's exposé. Players who interview Kabir before Naina/Rhea now have a concrete signal that Vikram was investigating multiple people, not just the Thakurs.
- **Naina → Rhea → Naina (return visit).** After Rhea confirms the channel sale, players can come back to Naina to ask follow-up questions ("did Vikram mention pulling Stripe statements?"). No new cue fires, but Naina has the full prior transcript and can layer in detail.
- **Any suspect asked about the Thakurs.** Deflections in round 2, but the deflection itself signals to attentive players that the Thakur thread matters. Primes round 3.

### Host fallback availability in round 2

`hostFallbackAfterTurns` thresholds are tuned per suspect:

- **Naina**: 4–5 turns. Easy to crack; host rarely needs to step in.
- **Rhea**: 8–10 turns. Players are supposed to work for this; only intervene if they're genuinely stuck.
- **Kabir**: 4–5 turns. He wants to talk.
- **Devraj, Bisht, Anya**: 999 in round 2 (effectively never, because their conditions need round-3/4 evidence anyway — the host-fallback prompt won't appear).

## 10. The guilt map

One of the game's strengths is that "who is guilty?" isn't a simple binary. Every suspect carries a different kind of guilt:

| Suspect | Guilt category | Charges (in-fiction) |
|---|---|---|
| **Mr. Bisht** | Mastermind | Conspiracy to murder (×2) — Thakur family + Vikram |
| **Inspector Devraj** | Executor | Murder, conspiracy, criminal negligence, obstruction of justice |
| **Rhea Bhatia** | Tamperer | Evidence tampering, embezzlement |
| **Anya Devi** | Accessory (moral) | Accessory to the Thakur cover-up; inadvertently triggered Vikram's murder; ultimately brought the case to the CBI |
| **Prof. Kabir Iyer** | Coward (moral) | Withholding information; sent second anonymous letter that cracked the case |
| **Naina Kapoor** | Bystander (moral) | Nothing criminal; lied about whereabouts; might have saved a life |

The game's closing question to the players:

> Who is truly guilty? The man who gave the order? The man who swung the lathi? The woman who warned the killer? The woman who heard the cry and walked away? Or the victim himself, who couldn't resist pulling at threads that others had killed to bury?

## 12. Key locations

- **Camel's Back Road** — the 3km walking path that is the heart of the story. The sharp bend near Gun Hill point is the murder site — old rusted railing, steep drop into a ravine, no streetlights, heavy mist after sunset. This is where Vikram fell, and where Anya walked as the Grey Lady.
- **Thakur Cottage, Landour** — the abandoned colonial bungalow. Ransacked rooms frozen in time. Broken locks. The empty rifle mount in the Brigadier's study. A monument to a fifteen-year-old crime.
- **Vikram's cottage** — his rented home down the road from Thakur Cottage. Where Anya cleaned, where the laptop sat open, where Rhea came at 5 AM.
- **Royal Pines Hotel** — Bisht's luxury hotel. Built on former Thakur land. The private office with the rifle on the wall. The CCTV system with the convenient gap.
- **Lovely Omelette Centre** — the chai shop where Vikram and Kabir had their heated conversation at 6:30 PM. The receipt for two cutting chai is a key clue.
- **Mussoorie police station** — where Devraj logged himself as "on duty all evening." Where the Thakur case file has gathered dust for fifteen years.
- **Mussoorie bus stand** — where Anya caught the 9:15 PM bus. Her ticket proves she was still in town much later than she claimed to have left the cottage.
- **The Cedar Grove shortcut** — a narrow local path through cedar trees connecting Landour to Camel's Back Road. Only locals know it. Relevant because cedar needles found on Vikram's jacket don't match the vegetation at the murder site — suggesting someone who came via the grove was near him. (This is a subtle clue pointing to local knowledge, though in the final story it's Devraj who killed him, not someone from the grove.)

## 13. Design notes

### The two-case structure

The game's core innovation is that players think they're solving one murder (Vikram's) but discover they're actually solving two (the Thakur cold case). The second case explains the first. This creates a satisfying mid-game pivot when the Thakur connection emerges — suddenly the investigation's scope doubles and everything they've learned gets recontextualised.

### Red herrings vs. relevant secrets

Every suspect's secret is real and provable — there are no pure dead ends. Rhea really did tamper with evidence. Naina really was on the road. Kabir really did argue with Vikram. The red herrings aren't false clues; they're true clues that point to real wrongdoing that isn't murder. This is more satisfying for players than dead ends, because every thread they pull reveals something — just not always the thing they expected.

### The moral question

The game should end not with triumph but with a question. The guilt map in Section 10 is designed to provoke discussion among players after the solution is revealed. There's no "right answer" to who is most guilty — it's a conversation, and that conversation is the emotional payoff of the game.

### Family-friendly calibration

The Thakur robbery-murders are described as a historical event, never shown or re-enacted. Vikram's death is described in terms of the evidence (the lathi mark, the broken railing) rather than the act itself. The emotional weight comes from betrayal, loyalty, and moral compromise — not from violence. A 10-year-old can engage with "this person lied" and "this person was afraid" without encountering anything disturbing.

### The Grey Lady as atmosphere

The Grey Lady thread serves a dual purpose: it creates an eerie, distinctly Mussoorie atmosphere that makes the game feel rooted in its setting, and it provides a crucial evidentiary thread. The supernatural framing should be maintained through most of the game — let players wonder — before resolving into something human and heartbreaking in the final act.
